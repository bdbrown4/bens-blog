Three weeks ago I spent a weekend in a hospital bed on a Dilaudid drip, unable to eat or drink, waiting to find out whether a gallstone was going to pass on its own or whether they'd have to go in and get it.

I could have avoided most of that. The warning signs had been showing up for five years. I just kept deciding they weren't worth paying attention to.

## The part I ignored

It started about five years ago — sharp, stabbing pains in my upper *right* abdomen. In hindsight the pattern was obvious: it was always after a greasy, unhealthy meal. At the time I never made the connection. The pain always subsided, so I carried on without any real concern.

Eventually those pains went away. I didn't pay much attention to that either.

What replaced them, a little over a year ago, was intense sharp pain in my upper *left* abdomen. Same routine — wait for it to pass. But this time I did start making the connection that unhealthy meals, pizza in particular, kicked these events off.

Here's what was actually happening, which I only understand now: the right-side pain was gallbladder attacks. When those stopped and moved left, it wasn't the problem going away. It was the problem getting worse. I had started passing gallstones, and they were getting stuck on the way out and causing acute pancreatitis.

The symptom didn't disappear. It relocated. And I read the relocation as an all-clear.

## Wednesday: pizza and a Guinness

I have a Wednesday night Pokémon TCG league with some friends. We play at a shop called Shuffler's Den in Willoughby — fantastic little shop, I highly recommend checking it out. We always do dinner after league wraps up, and that night we went to the Wild Goose in downtown Willoughby.

They're known for their pizza. How could I resist? I had a Guinness to pair with it. It was delicious.

That's when the pain started. I felt it sitting at the table with everyone. I made an excuse that I was done, wished them all a goodnight, and started toward my car. In the past, walking had helped, so I took a lap around the square to see if it would take the edge off.

It did not.

I got home as fast as I could and went straight for the shower. Scalding hot water helped things subside enough that I eventually made it to bed.

## Thursday: working through it

I woke up feeling terrible, but the pain wasn't as severe. So, naturally, I decided to work.

As the day went on I got worse. The pain started radiating again — upper left — and I logged off early in the afternoon. Looking back I'm genuinely surprised I made it that far into the day.

That evening I started vomiting. At one point that night I was in the fetal position on my bathroom floor.

## Friday: the ER

I woke up Friday still in pain, and somewhere in there I figured something probably wasn't right. But what actually moved me was Katie saying, flat out, that I needed to go to the ER. Hearing someone else say it out loud was the validation I apparently needed.

I drove to UH TriPoint in Concord Township, about fifteen minutes from the house. I got there around 7 AM and I was the only person in the ER.

I told the nurse at the front desk what was going on, and they did not waste time. I was rushed to the back, which honestly surprised me. It made me think I'd been downplaying my symptoms — which, with what I know now, I absolutely had been.

They started me on morphine immediately. I don't think I've ever been on a painkiller that strong in my life.

A CT scan, then an ultrasound, and finally a briefing: my gallbladder was effectively toast, and I'd likely been passing gallstones for a long time. They were getting stuck in my common bile duct and causing pancreatitis.

Okay, I thought. So what now? Am I getting surgery here?

Nope. TriPoint doesn't do that surgery — and neither does much of anywhere else in Northeast Ohio. UH St. John Medical Center in Westlake is what people around here call the GI capital of Cleveland. So I waited for transport to take me an hour west.

## My first ambulance ride

I felt like I could have walked to the ambulance. Liability said otherwise, so I got the stretcher and a rather grandiose exit from TriPoint, waving goodbye to the staff who'd taken care of me all day.

The ride to Westlake was bumpy. It honestly felt like it took a decade. Same stretcher, same grandiose entrance at St. John.

Once I got to my room I remember thinking: *finally, I'll get this surgery and be home by tomorrow.*

Wrong.

Because a stone could still be lodged in the common bile duct, the standard approach is to wait two days and see whether it passes on its own. I wasn't going anywhere. Weekend shot.

## The waiting

Katie has family on the west side, and her grandparents came in to see me along with her. Having people there while I was stuck made a real difference. We joked a lot, and it took my mind off the fact that I wasn't leaving anytime soon.

The plan: wait two days, then do an MRCP — a Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography, which is an MRI targeted at the bile ducts and the pancreatic duct.

Here's the anatomy that finally made this click for me. Bile leaves the liver and gallbladder and travels down the common bile duct toward the small intestine. Right at the end, that duct meets the pancreatic duct and they share a single outlet into the intestine. That shared exit is the whole problem: a gallstone parked there doesn't just block bile, it backs up the pancreas too. Which is why a gallbladder problem turns into pancreatitis.

If the stone was still stuck, I'd need an ERCP before the gallbladder could come out. That's an endoscopic procedure — they run a scope down the esophagus, through the stomach, into the small intestine, and back up into the duct through that shared opening to pull the stone out manually.

So: wait two days and find out.

Meanwhile I got "upgraded" to a Dilaudid drip. For anyone unfamiliar, it's considerably stronger than morphine. Learning that was on the table for me was its own kind of information about how bad things actually were.

It made me reflect on the pain. I've always said I have a high pain tolerance. I guess this justified it? Every few hours a nurse would come by and ask what my pain was at. I'd say six or seven, thinking that wasn't so bad — and get scolded every time, because six or seven is not a number you're supposed to be sitting on quietly. One nurse finally told me to quit being brave and use the medication when the pain came back.

By this point it had been about four days since I'd eaten. I couldn't eat or drink anything. That was the worst part of the whole experience, and I don't think it's close. It made me feel like I wasn't entirely there — like the situation was something my mind had made up. Although I suspect the Dilaudid was contributing to that particular feeling.

## Sunday: Yacht Rock and a lucky break

Sunday morning it was finally time for the MRCP. My first MRI, and a genuinely interesting experience. They hand you headphones and ask what you want to listen to so the noise isn't as bothersome.

I picked Yacht Rock Radio. Katie and I have been defaulting to that station for years — an homage to Hopper, our beloved little dog who we listened to it with often before he passed. Nice thing to have with me in that tube.

Things picked up fast after that. I'd been told I might be waiting until Monday for surgery at the earliest. An hour after the MRCP, they confirmed the stone had passed on its own — and the surgery team was in the building and could take me within the hour.

No ERCP. No waiting until Monday. Gallbladder out that day. I was thrilled.

The other good part: I knew that by the time I was out, I'd get to see Archie and Hugh for the first time since Friday morning. I was missing them.

## Surgery, and after

Surgery was a blur — and by blur I mean a blip. I went from lying on the table getting "oxygen" to waking up in the PACU with ice chips. Anesthesia is deeply strange to me.

Back to my room, back on pain meds. Katie, the boys, and my in-laws came by shortly after. I could tell Archie and Hugh were confused and a little scared to see me like that. I was split — thrilled to see them, and feeling terrible that they were seeing me in such a strange state and environment. They didn't stay long; bedtime was coming.

That night was uneven. The pain came and went, and I was uneasy after the operation. But the nursing staff wanted me mobile fast, and that part I was ready for. I've taken to walking a lot more in my personal life, so I got up and did a good number of laps around the floor right off the rip. I think it surprised them a little. It felt good.

By the next day I was on a regular walking schedule, and some of the doctors on the floor started doing laps with me. We got to know each other — they asked about my work, I asked about theirs. Turns out talking shop about AI with people who work in medicine is a great way to pass a hospital afternoon.

Katie came to take me home around 3 PM, and I was more than ready. One problem: the doctor who had to sign my discharge was nowhere to be found for most of the day. If they didn't turn up, I was there another night and Katie had driven out to Westlake for nothing. I was agitated, to put it mildly.

They rolled in right around 5 PM, did the paperwork, and we were free.

I walked all the way to the car. Felt good doing it. Was definitely overdoing it.

I got home right at the boys' bedtime, so I didn't see them that night. But I got a full night's sleep in my own bed, and I was back with my family.

## What I'd tell past me

Don't ignore your body.

I know how that sounds. So let me be more specific about what I actually got wrong, because "listen to your body" wasn't useful advice to me five years ago either:

I treated *recurring* pain as a series of unrelated one-offs. Each attack ended, so each one closed the file. I never zoomed out and saw five years of the same thing.

I treated a symptom moving as a symptom improving. It wasn't. It was the problem escalating into a different organ.

And I under-reported pain to the people whose entire job was to treat it. A six or seven meant something to that nurse that it didn't mean to me, and my toughing it out wasn't helping anyone — least of all me.

If any of that sounds familiar, go get it looked at. It's a lot cheaper than an ambulance ride and a lost weekend.
