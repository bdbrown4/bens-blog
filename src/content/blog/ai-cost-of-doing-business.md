### The Question I Keep Getting Asked

This one keeps finding me — at work, outside of work, from colleagues, from people who just know I'm "the AI guy." The shape of it changes but the core never does: *how do we justify the cost of AI, and how do we even measure the value it's delivering versus not using it at all?*

My brain spirals when I sit with this. So let me try to unpack a few threads.

### We've Been Here Before

Cloud is the example I keep coming back to. A decade or so ago, enterprises were wrestling with the exact same framing: do we justify migrating workloads off our own infrastructure? What does cloud actually deliver that we can't do in-house? The ROI debates were loud, the math was murky, and the resistance was real.

Worth being honest here: cloud ROI debates haven't fully gone away. Some organizations are actively pulling workloads back on-prem as usage costs scaled beyond what they anticipated. It never cleanly resolved the way we sometimes remember it.

But here's what *did* happen. The question quietly shifted. Cloud stopped being something you had to defend and started being something you had to explain *not* having. The cost of implementation became a necessity to remain competitive, full stop. Nobody bats an eye at an AWS bill the way they once did.

### The Edge Compounds Whether You Measure It or Not

Yes, AI is expensive right now. A lot of what's accessible today is subsidized to drive adoption — that's real. But subsidized or not, the competitive pressure is already here.

If your competitors are using AI to sharpen their products, accelerate their workflows, and close gaps faster, it doesn't matter how imperfect the ROI math is. In insurance — my world — the race to deliver better, faster, more precise solutions isn't slowing down because the tooling is hard to justify on a spreadsheet. That edge accumulates quietly and shows up later in ways that are hard to trace back to any single decision.

### The Part Nobody Wants to Hear About Metrics

There's always a push to map intangible value to a clean, targeted metric. And honestly? In a true sense, that clean metric doesn't really exist.

You can construct one. People do it constantly. But my time doing data analytics in grad school left me with one durable lesson I keep returning to: *correlation does not yield causation*. That's not just a stats disclaimer — it's a warning about the self-fulfilling nature of metrics. When you define what gets measured, you bake in your assumptions about what matters. The metric can have merit. It often does. But it's also a reflection of what you decided to look for, not necessarily what's true.

So I hold that tension when people push me to "prove" the value of AI investments. The ask is reasonable. The premise is shakier than it looks.

### So What Am I Actually Saying?

I think AI expenses will be inevitable — and I think we need to be prepared for the possibility that they become another cost of doing business, the same way cloud did. When that tipping point arrives and how AI evolves between now and then, I genuinely don't know. No one does.

But I do believe the direction is set. My hope is that we're not so caught up trying to quantify something that resists clean quantification that we miss the actual moment we're in. Something real is being built here. The way we operate, build, and deliver is changing in ways that will probably look obvious in hindsight.

I'd rather be a part of shaping that than be left explaining why I waited for perfect ROI data first.
