Last week I flew out to Colorado Springs for the AI Operations Initiative we've been pushing forward. I went in expecting a normal work trip. I came home with a cough, a much bruised reputation for rental car taste, and a few genuine insights about where this AI moment is actually taking us.

## Wheels Up, Cough Drops Down

The flight left around 11AM EST, packed with familiar faces — my old boss, a fellow AI enthusiast I lean on constantly, and a handful of C-level and senior leaders headed out for our CIO's in-person townhall that same week. Not a bad way to start a trip.

Less good: the toddler coughing away in the row next to me for most of the flight. I already had a small cough going in. I knew exactly how this was going to end, and I was right.

We landed around 12:30PM MST, and I made a beeline for baggage claim and then Enterprise for my rental. They always move fast for me, and this trip was no exception.

## The Mini Cooper, Again

Here's where it gets good. Last year, when I was still on the Operations team, Enterprise handed me a Q5 Audi — on the base plan, for the record, it was genuinely all they had left that week. I thought nothing of it until my peers found out and would not let it go.

Fast forward to this trip. I walk up to the counter, and sitting right there is a Mini Cooper convertible. I could already hear the commentary forming before I'd even signed the paperwork. Go-kart mode got me to the hotel fast, at least.

We stay at Hotel Polaris, right next to the Air Force base — genuinely a nice property. Check-in was smooth, and this time I made sure to get my receipt emailed (they confirmed there's a checkbox on their end for that, so future-me is set). On the way up, I stopped at the little lobby shop — coffee, snacks, and meds — and grabbed a bag of Halls. I had a feeling I was going to need them.

## Early Starts, Long Days

The first night was a good one. Everyone flew in, we watched the World Cup round-of-16 elimination game together — Belgium showed the US no mercy that night — then wound down with a few rounds of Flip 7 and Avalon before calling it.

I stayed on Eastern time the whole week, both for meetings and to keep some rhythm with my family back home. That meant 6AM starts and not leaving the office until 5–5:30 MST most days. Long days, and with the kids off the table in the evenings anyway, I leaned into the extra hours to get ahead — even though "ahead" isn't really a real state in this role anymore.

One of the things I genuinely love about Progressive is how accessible senior leadership is. I had several engaging, unscripted conversations with senior leaders throughout the week, and a few even wandered into the conference room my old boss had booked, just to see what the Operations team was up to with the AI initiative. And yes, the Mini Cooper followed me to dinner too. I knew I wasn't going to hear the end of it, and I didn't.

## AI Inception

This was the real point of the trip: working with my old team — the Operations team, who I still feel very much a part of — to get Claude Code set up on their personal machines.

The stated goal was an autonomous agent to offload some of the trivial day-to-day work. My actual goal was giving everyone that "epiphany" moment — the point where AI tooling stops being a novelty and starts feeling like a superpower baked into your daily workflow.

We already had GitHub Copilot in VS Code, so I found an existing onboarding doc, fed it in as context, and then had everyone use Copilot to help configure Claude Code. AI, setting up AI. Inception, basically — and it landed exactly the way I hoped. You could see it click for people that we were actually doing this.

Naturally, we hit configuration hiccups, different for nearly everyone. The fix was always the same: feed the error back to Copilot's model and let it work the problem. One peer told me afterward that they'd noticed me walking the room like a teacher checking on students — they'd only asked one question the entire session, right at the start, and then had their own breakthrough. Hit a wall, feed it to the LLM, guide it to resolution, repeat. A loop.

Is that agentic programming, distilled? A giant "Ralph Wiggum" loop, as the internet's been calling it? Maybe. But that peer is the part that matters. They've since told me they're fully passing commands to AI to clear their own roadblocks — cutting the toil out of their job so they can focus on the part they actually love.

That's the paradigm I actually believe in: keep the human engaged with the solution while they orchestrate a suite of commands into the agentic system underneath them. You'll hear a lot right now about "AI native autonomy" or "AI supervised autonomy" — fully hands-off systems — and I think that framing is a little ludicrous. Taking the human out of the loop entirely is a real detriment, and I'd bet a lot of organizations chasing that quietly walk it back once they feel what it costs their operations and culture.

My peer hit the first rung of that ladder this week. That's the whole goal — grow that mindset in my old team and hope it spreads outward across the org. My old boss and I are putting together an enterprise-accessible testimonial on it, and while the autonomous agent itself is part of the pitch, the real win is the adoption and the learning we instilled in the people. That's what actually moves an enterprise forward.

## Dim Sum, Pinball, and a Gut Check

The rest of the trip had its own highlights. We hit a Dim Sum spot where two different people ordered for the whole table without checking with each other — we ended up with double of everything. Then a pinball bar, which turned into some genuinely good one-on-one time with peers.

One conversation stuck with me. A peer pulled me aside and admitted they'd gone down a dark corner of Reddit and had themselves half-convinced AI was going to take everyone's jobs — to the point they'd started researching careers that felt "AI-proof." They said they'd since talked themselves back to a more grounded place, but it had clearly rattled them. I was glad they trusted me enough to bring it to me. I told them plainly: AI isn't a replacement, it's a force multiplier, and I meant it. I made sure they knew the door's open any time they need to talk it through.

The other conversation was with a group manager — my old boss's boss, someone I'd honestly always found a little intimidating. After a genuinely good back-and-forth that night, that feeling mostly evaporated. They also gave me feedback on my new role as AI Capability Leader that I'm still chewing on: it's not just about solutioning. It's about organizational impact — getting the broader org up to speed and maturing our adoption across the board, not just shipping the next clever thing.

That one landed. I'm a solutionist at my core, and I know it. The funny part is I didn't clock it in the moment, but reflecting on it back at the hotel that night, the Claude Code work with my old team was already, without me fully realizing it, a step in that direction.

## The Long Way Home

The CIO's in-person townhall happened that week too, and his team fielded questions well. People got real face time with IT Business Leaders in the breakout rooms afterward — I didn't make it to one, but I talk with my own IT Business Leader about AI regularly anyway. That's another thing I appreciate about this place: I can have a genuinely open, engaged conversation with someone that senior without a shred of hierarchical pressure hanging over it.

My flight home wasn't until 2PM MST, so I got an extra night at the hotel, worked most of the morning, and drove to Denver. Once I got to the airport, I picked work back up at the gate and in the terminal. There's something I actually enjoy about that — a list in front of me, figuring out the most efficient path through it, with Claude Code and Codex doing the heavy lifting alongside me.

The flight back was easy, and my seatmate this time was, thankfully, not sick. Uber picked me up, and I walked in the door around 8:15PM EST. The boys were already asleep, but I'll take that — I was eager to see them in the morning.
